Basics of Insight Meditation 02 (2025)

Bhante Bodhidhamma 34:20 Dharma Talks
Source: satipanya.org.uk

In this talk, Bhante Bodhidhamma traces the Buddha's spiritual journey from his departure at age 29 through his experiments with jhāna masters and severe asceticism, to the pivotal memory that transformed his approach. Rather than trying to escape suffering, the Buddha turned toward it with the investigative curiosity he recalled from childhood—a direct, pre-conceptual way of observing that became the heart of vipassanā meditation.

Bhante explains the Buddha's three models for understanding human experience: the five khandhas (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness), the six sense bases, and paṭicca samuppāda (dependent origination). He explores the Anattā teaching from the Buddha's second discourse, clarifying that 'not-self' doesn't deny we have a self, but invites investigation into whether any permanent, controllable self can actually be found. The three characteristics—impermanence, unsatisfactoriness (dukkha), and not-self—become the lens through which liberation unfolds.

Drawing from the Kevaḍḍha Sutta, Bhante describes Nibbāna as a consciousness that is non-manifestive, boundaryless, and full of light—utterly beyond ordinary sensory experience yet accessible in every moment of clear awareness. He emphasizes that the five hindrances (desire, aversion, sloth, restlessness, and skeptical doubt) are not obstacles to eliminate but teachers that reveal the three characteristics when observed skillfully.

The talk concludes with practical guidance: abandon the notion of progress, remain engaged through mettā practice, and recognize that simply sitting in the position of the observer—aware yet not identified—is itself the process of liberation.

So if you can imagine what it was like for the Buddha to leave his family. He's twenty-nine, he's married, and he's just had a child born. It's not as though he made a sudden decision and he's off. Some thought that if you practiced the jhānas, these absorptions, that's where you ended up forever, in these heavens. Others were materialists — they just said, well, when you die, you die.

That's the last sense of self that has to be undone, you might say.

In the discourse on not-self, which is the second discourse that he gives according to the tradition — the first one is just laying out the teaching, which eventually formalizes into the Four Noble Truths — one person listening to that, one of his six companions, becomes what we call a stream-enterer. The next point he comes to is impermanence. He says, if this self is meant to be an eternal soul, an eternal spirit in our language, if it's so, it can't arise and pass away. It was really trying to point people to investigate whether there was this eternal soul. And it was this process of investigation which is the liberating factor. This is the path, you might say.
