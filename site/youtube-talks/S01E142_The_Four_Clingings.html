The Four Clingings (Upādāna)

Bhante Bodhidhamma 15:49 YouTube Talks
Source: YouTube

In this illuminating talk, Bhante Bodhidhamma examines upādāna, the eighth link in paṭicca samuppāda (dependent origination), literally meaning the fuel that keeps active processes alive. He explores the four types of clinging that perpetuate suffering: clinging to sensual pleasures (kāmupādāna), clinging to rites and rituals (sīlabbatupādāna), clinging to views and opinions (diṭṭhupādāna), and clinging to self-view (attavādupādāna).

Bhante explains how clinging to sensual pleasure involves all six senses, including mental phenomena, and leads to constant manipulation of our environment. He discusses the supportive versus liberating aspects of ritual practices, examines how attachment to views creates polarization in politics and relationships, and reveals the root clinging to self-view from which all other clingings arise.

Drawing on core Buddhist psychology, he shares the Buddha's crucial insight that identity follows wanting, not the reverse—a understanding that creates space for choice and wisdom. Practical antidotes include vipassanā meditation to see these processes clearly, restraint and fasting from compulsive behaviors, cultivating humility regarding our views, and practicing virtues that undermine selfishness. This teaching offers both theoretical understanding and practical guidance for loosening the grip of clinging in meditation and daily life.

So we're tackling this word upādāna. It's translated as clinging, sometimes grasping. It's important because it's the eighth link on dependent origination. Its literal meaning, according to the Pali dictionary, is that material substratum by means of which an active process is kept alive and going: fuel, supply, provision. So there are four of these clingings. The dharma is very good at lists, so we've got four of these. If we look at the first one, most of you will know this - it's the desire for sensual pleasure. But remember that there are six senses in the Buddhist teaching, and the sixth, of course, is the mind itself. So it means all our emotions, all our thoughts, etc., which is dependent usually on some physical base.

The problem is that, of course, when we're driven by this desire, it's never fulfilling. As soon as we are fulfilled, we rest for a little while and then we want some more. Chanting, beading, it's all good to keep the mind steady, to stop it wandering all over the place. So all these practices - actually, it's like sealing a particular relationship with the public rite of marriage. There's the christening or the naming ceremony. And there's burials. These are all rituals and very meaningful. But in themselves, unfortunately, they don't lead to liberation.

The final one, of course, is clinging to the view of self. And this is the root clinging. From this clinging, all clinging clings. And the problem with the self is it only sees the sensual world, and it only sees the sensual world as a place where it can make itself happy.

So you can't blame yourself for wanting to be happy in the sensual world. And we can also undermine it by just practising virtues. Anything that undermines that sense of ego, a sense of selfishness - generosity, love, compassion, sympathetic joy and so on. All these - when we are practising virtues, we must be undermining the sense of self. Simple as that.

And finally, just this core insight that the Buddha gives us about the self. He saw clearly that the identity comes after the wanting. And it's language that fools us. I say, "I want a coffee." Now, this isn't some airy-fairy free will. This choice is conditioned. It's conditioned by wisdom or lack of it. And what we want to know is, well, how does wisdom grow? Well, it's the very suffering that's being caused that makes us seek the end of suffering, which guides us towards wisdom. And that's how it works. Now this is a really amazing insight that the Buddha had into our psychology.

So there we have it: these four clingings.
